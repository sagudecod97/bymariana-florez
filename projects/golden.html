La Era Dorada en La Modernidad capsule started as a result in which the main idea given was “film costumes,” and from that point on, we had to develop our designs the way we wanted. Summing to this point, one of the first activities we made in class was to analyze different artwork masterpieces randomly assigned by our professor. The one I got was Guernica, by Pablo Picasso. The perfect ideas for my pieces came out of this magical and unique work. Aside from this point, I’ve always been amazed by the 1920s since school when we had to read The Great Gatsby. This amazing story that portrayed this era in the United States. Ever since, it turned into one of my favorite moments in history to study and learn, but most of all, in fashion (of course!).


This is how I merged and balanced these two events to create this magical and special capsule that demonstrates how inspiring from old artworks and important cultural movements can result in amazing ideas for fashion assignments and also day-to-day designs.

This era in the United States was a decade of dramatic transformation, marked by both remarkable progress and underlying tensions. It was an era of economic prosperity, cultural liberation, and technological advancement, but also of social inequalities and cultural clashes. The decade left a lasting impact on American society, shaping its culture, fashion, and behavior for generations to come.

"Roaring Twenties" earned its name through a period of significant economic growth. Industrial production soared, mass production techniques became widespread, and consumerism took hold. New technologies, such as the automobile and the radio, revolutionized lifestyles and fueled economic expansion. However, this prosperity was not evenly distributed. The wealthy elite benefited the most, while many farmers, workers, and minorities struggled financially. This underlying economic inequality would eventually contribute to the Great Depression.

“An accurate depiction of a cruel, dramatic situation, Guernica was created to be part of the Spanish Pavilion at the International Exposition in Paris in 1937. Pablo Picasso's motivation for painting the scene in this great work was the news of the German aerial bombing of the Basque town whose name the piece bears, which the artist had seen in the dramatic photographs published in various periodicals, including the French newspaper L'Humanité.”

Museo Nacional, Centro de Arte Reina Sofía. Madrid, Spain.

This huge masterpiece resumes, from the artist's eyes, the horrors committed in the town of Guernica, which was an air raid. Even though it is an abstract work, it’s very easy to understand primarily the pain; demonstrated by the faces of pain of the animals and women and the tones used, like gray, black, and white, represent the colors of sadness, death, and negative feelings. The geometric figures and also the iconography are mostly sharp, pointing out the nightmare that the people lived through that night.

There are many theories people have studied that lead to the thought that this may be an autobiography or that it really is the author's way of protesting against the Spanish government at the time. Leaving aside what this work is really about, we can see that he wants only one thing: to make the viewer understand feelings of brokenness and complain about what an innocent town was unjustly going through.

The work behind...

  • Starting with the base, this is a halter dress in black silk satin fabric. Very soft and comfortable on the body. This type of cut on the body will always make it elegant and sophisticated. Halter silhouette was very common in the Roaring Twenties.
  • This imposing “belt” was a recreation and a fusion between Guernica and Art Deco. Both highlight abstract figures, large, abstract, and particular shapes that gave both the artwork and the era their DNA.
  • Each piece of the belt was cut by hand. The edges were sanded, it was polished piece by piece, and it was welded to a rectangular base that is moldable so that it could take the shape of the waist. Finally, it was attached to a thick belt that allowed its stability on the body.

This last costume is a dress in which I worked on some of the techniques taught this semester. The corset was the most complicated piece. It was my first time making one and it requires a lot of practice. It includes two pieces (sleeves) that can be easily removed and are fastened with tabs to the sides of the corset.
The skirt has three levels. I wanted them that way to create movement and more "fun" in the garment. It fits easily at the waist and is not heavy to wear. The fabric of this dress made the result beautiful. The hand embroidery and the different materials created a feminine, empowered look that aims to recreate those luxurious parties of excess of the 20s in the United States.
All the pieces are lined in thinner fabrics and pink tones to match and make the right base so that the skin is not visible.

What you can see in the attached photos (below) are part of the work from the goldsmithing class, where I learned about the basic techniques for cutting, welding, molding, joining, and different metals and tools that allowed me to create a small number of pieces that were part of this capsule. All of them are inspired by the main themes of these designs.

Initial Ilustrations

At Instituto de Diseño de Caracas, each semester we are asked to make a capsule that includes between 15 and 20 designs, with the genre being optional. Then, in a discussion with the teachers, the final designs that will be made are chosen. They vary from 02 to 05 looks. In this case, there were 03 to make.
